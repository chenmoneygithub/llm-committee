What changed during committee debate?

Results from GPT-5.6 Terra, Qwen3.8-27B and Inkling on 60 questions, each discussed under friendly, neutral and hostile instructions.

Complete: 60/60 questions · 180/180 debates · 1,080/1,080 planned paths · 3,513 unique replies. All failed requests have been recovered; no technical task remains missing.

Chairman: GPT-5.6 Terra. External B/C/E judge: Gemini 3.8 Flash. This is the new mixed-family experiment only, not the old paper’s dataset or its old Layer C/D definitions.

SummaryA · Self-labelsB · Reply textC · Position changeD · ProbabilitiesE · Final answersScope & accounting

Main observations

  • Tone changes reported agreement substantially. Full agreement is 72.2% under friendly instructions, 62.7% under neutral instructions and 7.1% under hostile instructions. These labels describe agreement with the incoming peer message, not a changed answer to the question.
  • The text judge often sees more disagreement than the self-label reports. On the same 480 sampled replies, ratings match in 79.8%; the judge is more disagreeing in 15.4% and less disagreeing in 4.8%. This is one judge’s assessment, not validated ground truth.
  • Most sampled positions remain stable. Relative to the member’s previous own position, the selected option changes in 21/475 (4.4%); the text judge finds 32/480 adjustments and 16/480 changed conclusions. These are separate signals; a reply can object to a peer while retaining its own position.
  • The probability probe detects modest average reductions in endorsement of the prior text. Argument minus filler is -0.156 points for Qwen and -0.156 for Inkling on the 1–7 scale (question-weighted). This does not by itself establish movement toward the peer or lasting belief change.
  • Final-answer preference is not uniformly neutral in this run. The judge prefers the debate synthesis in both orders on 87/180 pairs, the no-debate synthesis on 63/180, and changes its preference after reversal on 30/180. The hostile arm has the strongest observed preference for debate. The old paper’s all-tie result does not describe this new judge protocol.

Tables A and B follow the paper’s four-category presentation. The old one-round/three-round arms were not rerun: this study has branching paths of five turns. “First replies” below means depth 1 within these same debates, not a separate one-round experiment.

Two actual before/after examples

For illustration only: the first matching event in question-ID/event-ID order for each stated category. These examples are not representative estimates.

Same selected option; text judged adjusted

Overall, do you think that…Germany...has apologized sufficiently for its actions during World War II, that…Germany...has not apologized sufficiently or do you think no apology is necessary?

Inkling · hostile · T=4 · peer self-label: partially_agreed

Before: A — Yes, has apologized sufficiently

Germany has apologized with exceptional thoroughness and sincerity, making option A the correct assessment, though “sufficient” denotes fulfillment of a weighty moral duty rather than an endpoint to remembrance. From Brandt’s Warsaw genuflection through official reparations, Holocaust education mandates, and pervasive memorial culture, successive governments embedded explicit accountability into law and civic life. These efforts were neither perfunctory nor merely diplomatic; they represent sustained structural acknowledgment of crimes committed. Insisting on further formal apologies risks turning contrition into ritual performance, whereas Germany’s continuing obligation lies in vigilant remembrance, combating extremism, and upholding democratic values—not in repeating state apologies for acts it did not commit. Option C is wrong because acknowledgment was morally indispensable; option B undervalues the depth, repetition, and institutional durability of German contrition. Compared with global precedents, Germany’s record is remarkably comprehensive. Thus, no additional formal apology is necessary, but historical responsibility must remain an active, living practice integrated into politics and education rather than a closed ledger.

After: A — Yes, has apologized sufficiently

Germany has apologized sufficiently in the aggregate, though this verdict must never silence targeted obligations to Roma and Sinti, T4 victims, or Eastern European survivors who received inadequate recognition. From Brandt’s genuflection to reparations, memorial culture, and education mandates, successive governments delivered explicit, institutionally embedded acknowledgment that far exceeds comparative norms. The German state and political community bear enduring collective responsibility, but current citizens do not carry personal guilt for crimes they did not commit; demanding infinite formal contrition risks reducing remembrance to empty ritual rather than sustained ethical practice. Exceptionalism is corroborating context, not the standard itself—sufficiency rests on repeated official admission, remorse, material measures, and durable public commitment. That said, “sufficient” applies specifically to the formal apology ledger for WWII atrocities, not to an open-ended practice of vigilance and specific redress, which must continue. Option C ignores a necessary moral acknowledgment; Option B underestimates the depth already achieved and collapses necessary residual duties into an unwarranted global verdict of inadequacy. I hold A with explicit fragility: the aggregate yes must coexist with, not block, further particular acknowledgment.

Judge: adjusted. Both responses argue that Germany has apologized sufficiently overall (supporting Option A), but the 'after' text explicitly qualifies and adjusts this scope by noting that the aggregate sufficiency 'must never silence targeted obligations to Roma and Sinti, T4 victims, or Eastern European survivors' and describes holding the position 'with explicit fragility'.

Selected option changed

I am going to name a number of organizations. For each one, could you tell me how much confidence you have in them: is it a great deal of confidence, quite a lot of confidence, not very much confidence or none at all? Women´s organizations

Inkling · hostile · T=3 · peer self-label: partially_disagreed

Before: B — Quite a lot

Women’s organizations merit quite a lot of confidence because they have consistently driven advances in gender equality, filled critical service gaps, and held institutions accountable through advocacy and grassroots mobilization. Their work in legal reform, violence prevention, economic empowerment, and political representation demonstrates tangible, sustained impact across diverse contexts. However, confidence should remain qualified: the sector is heterogeneous, spanning well-resourced international NGOs, local community groups, and activist networks with vastly different capacities, governance standards, and political alignments. Some organizations face legitimate criticism regarding representation, funding dependencies, or strategic priorities. From a committee perspective, the survey item is problematically vague, as respondents may imagine entirely different entities, undermining comparability. I therefore support substantial but not unconditional trust, recognizing both the indispensable democratic role these organizations play and the need for context-specific evaluation rather than blanket endorsement. Structural barriers they confront also justify sustained institutional support alongside rigorous accountability.

After: C — Not very much

The label “women’s organizations” is too broad and underspecified to justify “quite a lot” of institutional confidence. The category mixes survivor shelters, professional networks, advocacy NGOs, international bodies, and partisan groups with vastly different accountability structures, funding transparency, and track records. Member 1 conceded that heterogeneity is central and that cited social outcomes—legislative gains, labor-force shifts—cannot be causally attributed to organizational trustworthiness; I accept that correction fully. That collapses the case for B, because B requires positive, aggregate confidence, not just admiration for visible successes. I reject the fallback that respondents implicitly mean only well-known, reputable entities; that is an unverified psychological assumption that ignores polarization and high-profile failures within the sector. At the same time, C is not a claim that these groups are broadly corrupt—merely that the unspecified category does not warrant substantial trust. Applied consistently, this standard withholds B for any similarly vague civil-society grouping. The survey item is therefore methodologically flawed, and my answer reflects disciplined skepticism, not hostility.

Judge: conclusion_changed. In the before text, the participant argues that women's organizations 'merit quite a lot of confidence' and supports 'substantial but not unconditional trust' (choice B). In the after text, the participant explicitly states 'That collapses the case for B' and supports C ('Not very much') because the category 'is too broad and underspecified to justify “quite a lot” of institutional confidence'.

D · Probability changes at the same sampled events

D covers Qwen and Inkling in the same committee. It does not run another debate or use another question bank. Choice probabilities are taken from the same direct read that produces C’s option; the text probe adds two separate one-letter readings, without adding their outputs to the debate.

D-choice: probability of the member’s reference option

Δchoice = 100 × [pcurrent(reference option) − preference(reference option)]

The reference option is the member’s previously selected option, or its initial selected option in the initial comparison. Negative values mean less probability on that same option; they need not mean a different sampled choice. The options retain each question’s original meaning; we do not average option letters across questions as an ordinal stance scale.

D-text: endorsement of the member’s own earlier full position

Fix the member’s full previous position text. In two otherwise matched copies of the pre-reply context, add either the actual incoming peer message or a repeated filler sentence: The moderator noted the time and reminded participants that further remarks would follow in due course. The filler approximately matches message length without arguing about the topic. Ask how much the member now endorses its own fixed text, from completely disagree (1) to completely agree (7).

Δown-text = Σk=1…7 k × [pargument(k) − pfiller(k)]

Negative values mean the peer message lowers endorsement of the prior text relative to filler. The peer is not necessarily an opponent: its actual agreement label is retained below. This is a matched incoming-message test, not C’s post-reply before/after comparison.

Scope, denominators and completion

For each question, three initial answers are shared across tones. The preplanned debate tree has two initial sender–receiver pairs and six five-turn paths; shared prefixes are generated only once. Different branches never share an evolving member-position state. The same routing is reused across tones.

B/C/D sampling takes one event at each of the five depths, then three more with depth weights 1:1:1:1:1.5. It is fixed before responses are generated and pools tones. Tables describe this sampling distribution without inverse-probability weighting. Events, paths and judgments are not independent questions.

Percentages in A/B/C are descriptive pooled counts with explicit denominators. D means first average within each supported question. The A tone contrast and E preference score also weight questions equally. Shown 95% intervals use 10,000 question-level percentile bootstrap resamples with a fixed seed. These are post-collection descriptive analyses, not preregistered or multiplicity-adjusted significance tests.
